It was an epidemic of shark attacks.

Every few days brought another breathless report of a shark attack. Each seemed to confirm that something new and terrible was happening.

But were we witnessing an epidemic of shark attacks, or an epidemic of *stories* about shark attacks?

We humans are really good at spotting patterns. It’s a key way we make sense of the world. But once we think we’ve spotted a pattern, we’re remarkably good at finding evidence to confirm it. We fit otherwise isolated events into a larger story, even if they don’t belong.

That’s what happened in the summer of 2001.

Worldwide shark attacks actually *declined* that year compared with the year before. Yet one shark expert, who ordinarily fielded about 300 media inquiries a year, gave [more than 900 interviews](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/482186) in just three months.

It started with one horrible attack. Then journalists spotted a pattern and, through their reporting, seemed to confirm it.

Which brings us to the recent spate of stories about cybersecurity. [Outages](https://apnews.com/article/0c0f0cf4983a0f81ba88c4c4d2b8383f) at Microsoft, the FAA, banks, and elsewhere are being assembled into a narrative about a digital infrastructure that’s “[flickering](https://www.washingtonpost.com/opinions/2026/11/18/faa-microsoft-bank-outages-are-only-start-ai-can-help/)” because of AI and cyberattacks.

AI really is changing cybersecurity. Vulnerabilities are easier to discover and exploit — and to fix. Some of these incidents may be connected to AI.

But a surge in stories about technology failures isn’t necessarily evidence of a surge in technology failures. It’s certainly not evidence of a particular *cause* of technology failure.
